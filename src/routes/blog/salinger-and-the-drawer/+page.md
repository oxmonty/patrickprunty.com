J.D. Salinger published his last story in 1965 and then lived another
forty-five years. The part people repeat is the silence. The part that
interests me is that he apparently kept working the whole time, in a bunker
behind the house in Cornish, with a filing system for what was finished and
what was not.

So the story is not that he stopped. It is that he unhooked the writing from
the publishing, and kept only the half he liked.

## The unhooking

Most of us have the two welded together. You build the thing in order to ship
the thing, and if it is not going to ship you do not start it. That is a
reasonable way to run a business and a strange way to run a mind. It means
every idea has to pass an audience test before it gets to exist, and the
audience test is the least informative signal available at that stage.

Salinger ran it the other way around. Whether that made the late work good is
unknowable, which is the whole problem with the example.

## Where it stops being useful

The drawer protects you from criticism, and criticism is most of how the work
gets better. Forty-five years of unedited output is not obviously a triumph,
and the estate has been slow to publish it, which tells you something.

The version I can actually defend is smaller: keep two piles. One is for things
that have to survive contact with other people, and it gets shipped, reviewed,
and argued about. The other is for things that are still figuring out what they
are, and nobody sees it, including the part of you that wants to know if it is
any good yet.

The mistake is not having a drawer. It is having only a drawer.
